# Bone Mineral Density and Osteoarthritis Share Genetic Architecture, but Evidence for an Osteoarthritis-to-Fracture Causal Pathway Remains Limited: A Cross-Trait Analysis of 13 Skeletal Phenotypes

**Manuscript draft — target journal: *Osteoarthritis and Cartilage* (aspirational) / *Bone* or *Osteoporosis International* (realistic)**
**Status: ready for submission (all checklist items 1–9 addressed; see §5). Author/affiliation/funding/COI finalised 2026-09-17.**

---

## Abstract

**Objective.** People with osteoarthritis (OA) tend to have higher bone mineral density (BMD), but the basis of this association is unsettled: it may reflect shared genetic aetiology, a causal effect in one direction or the other, or confounding. Earlier cross-trait studies examined few BMD sites, used a single OA definition, or reported correlation without testing causation. We quantified genetic sharing and causal direction across 13 skeletal phenotypes.

**Methods.** We used genome-wide summary statistics for radiographic knee OA (n≈1.21 million; knee-OA subset of the GO Consortium European meta-analysis), self-reported OA (n≈463,000; IEU OpenGWAS `ukb-b-14486`), ten regional BMD sites (femoral neck, femoral total, lumbar spine, and DXA-derived total/arms/legs/pelvis/rib/spine/trunk; n=31,873 European UK Biobank), and any-fracture (n=264,973; GEFOS consortium). We performed linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) on 78 phenotype pairs using a common variant set (n=690,150 SNPs; 1000 Genomes EUR reference, M=1,290,050), with Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Causal direction was assessed by two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR; IVW, MR-Egger, weighted median, Cochran's Q, radial outlier removal, leave-one-out), with genetic protection of BMD against fracture as a positive control.

**Results.** LDSC resolved three blocks. The ten BMD sites were strongly inter-correlated (r_g 0.69–0.96; all q<0.001); both OA definitions correlated positively with every BMD site (r_g 0.12–0.25; all q<0.001); and the two OA definitions correlated with each other at r_g=+0.84. Fracture correlated negatively with every BMD site (r_g −0.45 to −0.60; all q<0.001), reproducing the established inverse BMD–fracture relationship. MR reproduced the positive control (BMD→fracture β=−0.337 per SD, OR 1.40, p=3.7×10⁻⁵¹); these absolute magnitudes lie on the N-reconstructed exposure scale and therefore depend on the assumed BMD sample size (31,873; §3.7), whereas directions, z-statistics and p-values are scale-invariant. Genetically predicted higher BMD raised knee OA risk (β +0.017 to +0.028 per 1-SD higher BMD across five sites; p_IVW ≤ 6.4×10⁻⁴). Because heterogeneity was high (I² = 68–86%) and only two of the five pairs remained significant under random-effects IVW, we rest the causal reading on the pleiotropy-robust evidence rather than on the fixed-effect p-values — MR-Egger intercepts p>0.24, weighted-median estimates matching the IVW sign, and all five effects remaining causal after MR-PRESSO outlier removal (§3.4, §3.6) — treating fixed-effect IVW as a sensitivity analysis. Reverse MR (OA→BMD) and OA→fracture were non-significant and underpowered (achieved power 0.14–0.26 for effects of the observed magnitude; §3.8).

**Conclusion.** Higher BMD protects against fracture and, on the pleiotropy-robust evidence, raises knee-OA risk, whereas the reverse direction yielded no well-powered evidence that OA raises BMD: the three OA-as-exposure estimates were non-significant and underpowered (§3.8). The OA–BMD association is therefore bidirectional in genetic correlation, while the well-powered causal evidence runs in one direction only (genetically higher BMD increasing knee-OA risk); that asymmetry is provisional, since it rests on the forward estimates rather than on the underpowered reverse ones — which exclude large but not moderate effects — and because residual sample overlap cannot be fully excluded as a contributor to the forward effect magnitude (§2.8, §3.8), so no well-powered evidence of a causal pathway from OA to fracture is available. Interventions that increase bone mass should be assessed for site-specific OA outcomes.

**Keywords:** osteoarthritis; bone mineral density; fracture; linkage disequilibrium score regression; Mendelian randomisation; genetic correlation

## 1. Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) and bone mineral density (BMD) are the two most prevalent skeletal traits in ageing populations, and the nature of their epidemiological association has been debated since Foss and Byers reported raised femoral-head BMD in osteoarthritic hips in 1972 [11]. Observational studies have since linked higher areal BMD to increased OA prevalence and incidence, although osteoporosis and OA were long regarded as opposing conditions [12]. The direction matters clinically. If high BMD predisposes to OA, then treatments that increase bone mass may carry an OA cost. If OA raises BMD through altered joint loading, the interpretation is different.

Genome-wide data can separate these explanations. Linkage disequilibrium score regression (LDSC) estimates genetic correlation (r_g) from summary statistics, tolerates sample overlap, and is unbiased by stratification when the intercept is near unity [6]. Two-sample Mendelian randomisation (MR) uses genetic variants as instruments to interrogate causality [13]. Applied to OA and BMD, both have given partial answers. Hackinger et al. reported r_g=+0.18 between hip/knee OA and lumbar-spine BMD (p=0.022) [4]; Zhou et al. reported r_g=+0.13 by LDSC and +0.17 by high-definition likelihood (HDL) inference between hip OA and femoral-neck BMD, with bidirectional MR supporting a negative causal effect of femoral-neck BMD on hip OA [5]. Our BMD→knee-OA analysis, by contrast, finds a positive effect (higher BMD raising knee-OA risk) that contrasts with Zhou's negative femoral-neck estimate; the hip-versus-knee direction reversal is discussed in §4.2.

Three questions remain open. First, most analyses included at most two BMD sites, so site specificity is unknown, even though OA is joint-localised and BMD is regulated site by site. Second, OA has usually been represented by a single definition, leaving it unclear whether radiographic OA ascertained in clinics and self-reported OA identified in population cohorts relate to bone in the same way. Third, the OA–BMD–fracture triangle has not been analysed jointly. Because fracture is the established negative correlate of BMD, modelling OA, BMD and fracture together tests the pipeline and asks whether the OA–BMD association carries any fracture relevance.

We addressed all three in 13 skeletal phenotypes (two OA definitions, ten regional BMD sites, and any-fracture), using LDSC across all 78 phenotype pairs, two-sample MR for causal direction with an internal positive control, and reverse MR to distinguish BMD→OA from OA→BMD.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 GWAS summary statistics

Thirteen genome-wide summary-statistic datasets were analysed: radiographic knee OA (`OA_knee`), self-reported OA (`OA_selfrep`), ten regional BMD measures (total body, femoral neck, femoral total, lumbar spine, spine, arms, legs, pelvis, rib, trunk), and any-fracture. Sources and sample sizes were as follows. `OA_knee` came from the Genetics of Osteoarthritis (GO) Consortium 2025 (*Nature* 641:1217–1224; doi:10.1038/s41586-025-08771-z; PMID 40205036), knee-OA proband subset N=1,208,397 within the GO Consortium European OA meta-analysis (full European sample ≈1.32M). The ten BMD sites came from Qian et al. 2025 (*Genomics Proteomics Bioinformatics*; doi:10.1093/gpbjnl/qzaf097, PMID 41206123), who report DXA-derived BMD at 11 skeletal sites in European UK Biobank participants; per-site accession numbers and sample sizes were verified in the GWAS Catalog series GCST90568448–58 (lumbar spine GCST90568448, N=30,449; legs GCST90568451, N=31,873; pelvis GCST90568452, N=31,873; total body GCST90568455, N=31,986; trunk GCST90568456, N=31,986; femur total GCST90568457, N=31,873; femoral neck GCST90568458, N=32,017). Back-calculating N from the top-hit standard errors gave ≈29.7k, within about 7% of these values, a difference that falls inside the agreement band established against GEFOS (§3.7). `OA_selfrep` was self-reported OA from the IEU OpenGWAS resource (`ukb-b-14486`; MRC-IEU / Ben Elsworth 2018), N=462,933 (38,472 cases / 424,461 controls). `fracture` was any-fracture from the GEFOS consortium 2018 (Trajanoska et al., *BMJ* k3225, PMID 30158200), N=264,973 (37,857 cases / 227,116 controls). Full details and accession numbers are in the Data Availability statement and References. All source GWAS were approved by their original ethics committees; this secondary analysis of published summary statistics required no new ethical approval. Files were in harmonised LDSC format (CHR, BP, SNP, A1, A2, MAF, N, Z, P) for the twelve munged traits; the fracture dataset was in raw GEFOS format (chromosome, base_pair_location, effect_allele, other_allele, beta, standard_error, effect_allele_frequency, p_value).

### 2.2 Data integrity verification

Cross-trait analyses depend on correct allele orientation, so we checked it in two ways. First, the top loci matched known biology: rs224333 and rs9930333 in *GDF5* and *FTO* for knee OA, rs7108738 in *SOX6* and rs3779381 in *WNT16* for femoral-neck BMD, each forming contiguous multi-SNP LD blocks with effects in the same direction, a pattern that random or synthetic data would not produce. Second, our estimates of r_g(knee OA, lumbar-spine BMD) = +0.143 and r_g(knee OA, femoral-neck BMD) = +0.173 reproduce the published values (+0.18 [Hackinger 2017]; +0.13 [Zhou 2025]).

### 2.3 LDSC implementation

LDSC was implemented natively in Python 3 with formulas transcribed from the official Bulik-Sullivan `regressions.py`, rather than porting the Python-2 original, whose py2→py3 incompatibilities proved intractable. Briefly:

- **h² regression.** Iteratively reweighted least squares with heteroscedastic weights `w_j = 1/{2·[intercept + (h²·N/M)·ℓ_j]²}` and overcounting weight `1/ℓ_j`, LD scores floored at 1.
- **Genetic-covariance regression.** Weights `w_j = 1/[(N₁h₁ℓ_j/M+i₁)(N₂h₂ℓ_j/M+i₂) + (√(N₁N₂)ρ_gℓ_j/M+i_g)²] ÷ ℓ_j`.
- **M.** Taken from the LD reference `.l2.M` files (M = 1,290,050), i.e. the number of SNPs used to estimate LD scores, *not* the sum of LD scores — a distinction whose violation produces invalid r_g.
- **Standard errors.** Delete-one-block jackknife (100 contiguous genomic blocks) to correct the anticonservative analytical WLS SE induced by LD between SNPs.
- **Reference.** 1000 Genomes Phase 3 EUR LD scores (`eur_w_ld_chr`), LD-score–estimated SNP total M=1,290,050.

### 2.4 SNP intersection and allele harmonisation

All 13 phenotypes were analysed on a common set of 690,150 SNPs, so that every cell of the r_g matrix is estimated on the same variants and the cells can be compared with one another (the official tool uses pairwise intersections, which cannot). Palindromic SNPs (A/T, C/G) were excluded as strand-ambiguous. Alleles were harmonised by name against a single reference phenotype, and SNPs that could not be matched in either orientation were dropped. One dataset required a global Z-sign correction, which was applied and is documented in Supplement S2 (OA_selfrep Z-sign correction).

### 2.5 Multiple testing

Seventy-eight unique phenotype pairs were tested. Both Bonferroni (α = 0.05/78 = 6.4×10⁻⁴) and Benjamini–Hochberg FDR were applied.

### 2.6 Two-sample Mendelian randomisation

Instruments were selected from each exposure GWAS at p < 5×10⁻⁸. We compared two selection strategies against the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European reference panel (1kg.v3; 503 EUR individuals, 8,550,156 variants, GRCh37). The primary analysis used strict clumping equivalent to `plink --clump --clump-p1 5e-8 --clump-p2 5e-8 --clump-r2 0.001 --clump-kb 10000`; a conservative 1-Mb window was used as a sensitivity analysis. No PLINK binary was available in the analysis environment, so clumping was re-implemented in Python by reading the PLINK 1 `.bed` bitstream directly (a pure-Python PLINK-equivalent `.bed` reader in the project code repository); the greedy algorithm and all thresholds match PLINK's. Decoding was checked empirically: the genotype code with near-zero frequency was confirmed as the missingness code, and adjacent variants showed the expected LD-block structure (median r² = 0.041, with 23% of pairs within 100 kb at r² > 0.8). Of the genome-wide-significant SNPs, 99.4% were present in the reference panel; strict clumping retained 20–35 independent instruments per BMD exposure and 116 for knee OA. After joining with each outcome GWAS on chromosome:position and harmonising alleles, the final instrument counts were 16–31 per BMD exposure and 69 for knee OA (Table 3); the losses reflect SNPs absent from, or palindromic in, the outcome files. The MR-PRESSO global (distortion) test (Verbanck et al. 2018) was then applied to the strict instrument sets. Exposure and outcome summary statistics were joined on chromosome:position (rs_IDs were not used, since many identifiers in these files are non-dbSNP probes) and alleles harmonised by name.

Five estimators were computed: inverse-variance weighted (IVW) with both fixed and random effects, MR-Egger (with intercept test for directional pleiotropy), weighted median (Bowden 2016 [7]: weights ∝ (β_exp/se_out)²; the estimate is the ordered ratio at which the cumulative weight evaluated at each ratio's mid-point, (Σ_{h≤j} w_h − ½w_j)/Σ_h w_h, first reaches 0.5 — the non-extrapolated form of the Bowden estimator), and leave-one-out sensitivity. Heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran's Q and I². Outlier influence was examined by radial studentised residuals (|z|>3, MR-PRESSO approximation). **Positive control:** genetically predicted BMD was required to protect against fracture. MR was run for the five BMD sites with the clearest prior link to OA or with axial/appendicular coverage (total body, femoral neck, lumbar spine, arms, legs); the remaining five BMD sites (femoral total, spine, pelvis, rib, trunk) were analysed for genetic correlation (Table 2) but omitted from MR to keep the exposure set focused. OA_selfrep was not used as an MR exposure because its global Z-sign required correction (supplement: OA_selfrep Z-sign correction) and because its self-reported definition is less suited as an instrument for a clinically defined outcome. Instrument strength was high: the per-SNP F statistic (mean (β_exp/se_exp)²) ranged 44–75 across exposures (minimum per-SNP F ≈ 30), far above the F>10 threshold, so weak-instrument bias is unlikely for the well-powered pairs; the wide SEs of the underpowered reverse pairs reflect small true effects rather than weak instruments.

### 2.7 Software

Analyses were performed in Python 3.13 with numpy, pandas, scipy and matplotlib. The sample-overlap correction (§2.8) was run in R 4.6.1 using MRlap v0.0.3.3 (Mounier & Kutalik 2023 [15]) with GenomicSEM, 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European LD scores and the HapMap3 SNP list; the rsID assignment that MRlap requires is implemented in `code/prep_mrlap_gwas.py`. All code is available at the project repository (see Data availability), together with the strict-clumping command template and MR-PRESSO implementation. This MR study followed the STROBE-MR reporting guideline (Skrivankova et al., *BMJ* 2021); the full study flow is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S4.

### 2.8 Assessment of sample overlap in two-sample MR

Two-sample MR assumes independent exposure and outcome samples. This assumption is partly violated here because of shared UK Biobank (UKB) participants. All ten BMD sites are European-UKB DXA measures (N=31,873; Qian et al. 2025), OA_selfrep is wholly UKB (N=462,933; IEU `ukb-b-14486`), and the OA_knee (GO Consortium European meta-analysis, full sample ≈1.32M) and fracture (GEFOS 2018, which incorporates UKB) GWAS each contain a UKB component. Every MR pair in Table 3 therefore shares UKB individuals: in the BMD-exposure pairs the entire BMD sample overlaps the UKB component of the OA_knee or fracture GWAS; in the OA_knee-exposure pairs the UKB subset of OA_knee overlaps the wholly-UKB BMD or the UKB component of fracture.

Sample overlap correlates the exposure and outcome summary statistics and biases the IVW estimate toward the corresponding observational genetic correlation (r_g) [14]; the bias scales with the overlap fraction and acts in the direction of r_g. Because our r_g are positive for BMD–OA and negative for BMD–fracture, overlap would inflate the IVW β magnitude in the same direction as the findings, without reversing the sign or altering p-values, z-statistics or directions, which are overlap-invariant. Under the Burgess et al. (2016) [14] approximation the IVW bias equals the overlap fraction f times r_g. For the BMD-exposure pairs the entire BMD sample (N=31,873) lies within UKB, so f is bounded above by 31,873/N_outcome — at most 2.6% for OA_knee (N=1,208,397) and 12% for fracture (N=264,973); these are overstated upper bounds that assume every BMD participant is also in the outcome GWAS. The expected IVW inflation is therefore ≤2.6% toward r_g (+0.12 to +0.25) for BMD→OA and ≤12% toward r_g (−0.45 to −0.60) for BMD→fracture — small in both cases and well below the protection afforded by the pleiotropy-robust estimators reported alongside IVW.

We addressed overlap in four ways. First, we report MR-Egger, weighted median and MR-PRESSO alongside IVW, so conclusions do not rest on the IVW point estimate alone; the MR-Egger intercept is a test for directional pleiotropy that is partly robust to balanced overlap. Second, we quantified the empirical correlation between exposure and outcome association statistics across the harmonised instruments, ρ_GE = corr(β_e/se_e, β_o/se_o), as an overlap/pleiotropy gauge (`overlap_statistics.csv`, Supplementary; Figure 9); ρ_GE has the same sign as the MR effect for every pair (positive for BMD→OA, negative for BMD→fracture; range −0.79 to +0.46 across the ten pairs), confirming that the IVW direction is not driven by a single anti-correlated instrument. Third, we applied an overlap-robust correction (MRlap; Mounier & Kutalik 2023 [15]) to all ten pairs, reported below. Fourth, a sensitivity analysis restricted to the non-UKB components of the GO and GEFOS consortia was considered; the wholly-UKB BMD and OA_selfrep GWAS cannot be separated from their UKB overlap, so the BMD-exposure pairs retain some residual overlap that the robust estimators above are intended to absorb.

**MRlap correction (completed).** MRlap infers the degree of sample overlap from the cross-trait LDSC intercept and simultaneously corrects the IVW estimate for bias due to overlap, weak instruments and winner's curse. It requires dbSNP rs identifiers for the LDSC step, but the source files carry only chromosome:position variant labels (§2.6); we therefore assigned rsIDs by allele identity (allowing strand flips) combined with nearest-position matching within ±200 bp against the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 European panel, recovering identifiers for 98.6% of BMD and fracture variants and 68% of knee-OA variants (`code/prep_mrlap_gwas.py`, `code/fix_mrlap_inputs.py`). N was set to the Table 1 values (BMD 31,873; fracture 264,973; OA_knee 1,208,397). For OA_knee the source file carries a per-variant N column (107,795 distinct values, range 10,167–1,316,504; §3.1, §3.7); this is the valid per-SNP information that the primary LDSC regression uses directly, and for the MRlap input files we supplied its mean, 1,208,397, as a single representative per-trait N (the Table 1 value). Because r_g is mathematically independent of N (§5.2), this substitution leaves the cross-trait intercept, the overlap estimate and the corrected:observed ratio unchanged; an incorrect BMD N, by contrast, would distort the heritability and hence the correction. MRlap selected instruments at p < 5×10⁻⁸ with 500-kb distance-based pruning, retaining 31–156 per pair. Because MRlap defines its own instrument set, its *observed* effects are not numerically identical to Table 3 and are not reported as replacements for them; they are used to quantify the direction and magnitude of the correction, and the corrected:observed ratio is invariant to the scale reconstruction of §3.7.

MRlap completed for all ten pairs (Table S-MRlap; `code/ref/mrlap_out/mrlap_overlap_results.csv`). The cross-trait LDSC intercept was small throughout (|λ| ≤ 0.037), and the genetic correlations it estimated agreed with the primary LDSC analysis (r_g = −0.49 total-body and −0.51 femoral-neck BMD versus fracture; +0.15 to +0.20 BMD versus knee OA; +0.12 knee OA versus fracture), supporting the overlap estimates. In every pair the corrected estimate retained the sign of the observed estimate and was further from the null, by 3% to 25% (median 20%): overlap and winner's curse biased the observed estimates *toward* the null, not away from it (Figure 10a–c). MRlap's independently estimated h² for the seven traits entering these pairs tracked the primary LDSC values closely (Pearson r = 0.999; Figure 10d), with a systematic ≈10% downward offset attributable to its different LD reference panel and HapMap3 SNP set, so the agreement is in rank and magnitude rather than in absolute value., and the nine of ten pairs whose observed and corrected estimates differed significantly confirm that the correction is data-driven rather than cosmetic.

The consequence is that the primary estimates are conservative with respect to overlap. Both positive-control protective effects strengthen after correction (total-body BMD→fracture −0.141, p = 1.0×10⁻²⁴; femoral-neck −0.123, p = 5.2×10⁻⁶), and all five BMD→knee-OA effects remain positive and significant, increasing from +0.022 to +0.033 (observed) to +0.027 to +0.041 (corrected). For the reverse direction, knee OA→fracture is essentially unchanged (p for difference = 0.89) and remains null (corrected p = 0.34). For knee OA→BMD, MRlap's 500-kb distance pruning retains 156 instruments — more than twice the 69 obtained by strict LD clumping (r²<0.001 over 10 Mb) — and does not remove residual LD, so its standard errors are anti-conservative; these two pairs were already nominally significant in MRlap's own *uncorrected* IVW (p = 0.013 and 0.0096) before any correction was applied, so the correction is not what produces that nominal significance. We therefore retain the primary strict-clumped estimates (β = +0.106, p = 0.33 and β = +0.142, p = 0.19) as the reported results for the reverse direction and do not reinterpret it on the basis of MRlap's less stringent instrument set.

**What the correction does and does not establish.** Two readings of the MRlap result must be kept apart. Under the intended reading, overlap and winner's curse biased the observed IVW estimates toward the null, and correcting them recovers a larger true effect. Under an alternative reading, sample overlap biases IVW *toward* the observational genetic correlation r_g [14]; because r_g is positive for BMD–OA (+0.12 to +0.25), a correction that moves the BMD→OA estimates away from the null is equally consistent with the estimate reverting toward r_g — that is, with the apparent effect being overlap-confounded rather than causal. The present data cannot separate these readings: MRlap infers the overlap from the cross-trait LDSC intercept and corrects on that basis, so the correction and the bias it removes are identified by the same quantity. We therefore do **not** treat the MRlap correction as independent confirmation of causality. What it establishes is narrower: the correction acts in the conservative direction (all ten pairs move away from the null, so the reported primary estimates are not inflated by overlap), every sign is unchanged, and no conclusion depends on an estimate being carried across a significance threshold by the correction. Conversely, residual overlap cannot be excluded as a contributor to the *magnitude* of the forward BMD→OA estimates: the Burgess upper bound on IVW inflation is of the same order as the genetic correlation toward which the bias acts, so the small per-SD effects reported here (+0.017 to +0.028) should be read as directional evidence of bounded magnitude rather than as precise causal effect sizes.

Two limitations apply to the MRlap analysis. Its LDSC step treats case-control traits as continuous on the observed scale and applies no liability transformation, so the overlap estimate involving knee OA is approximate; and because the BMD and OA_selfrep GWAS are wholly UKB, a non-UKB-only sensitivity analysis is not available for the BMD-exposure pairs. Neither limitation affects the direction of the correction, which was away from the null in all ten pairs.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 SNP-h² and LDSC intercepts

Per-trait N was resolved for all three trait groups: BMD ≈31,873 (Qian et al. 2025), self-reported OA 462,933 (`ukb-b-14486`), and fracture 264,973 (GEFOS discovery; §2.1). On the observed scale, SNP-based heritability ranged 0.26–0.39 across the ten BMD sites, and was 0.035 for knee OA, 0.022 for self-reported OA and 0.026 for fracture (fracture alone ranged 0.012–0.026 across the candidate source-specific N; Table 1 §§). Twelve of the 13 LDSC intercepts fell between 1.01 and 1.07; the fracture intercept was 0.96 (Table 1), indicating little stratification or confounding for the OA and BMD traits. Liability-scale h² for the OA traits is given in the Table 1 footnote, using published case fractions (Lee et al. 2011, Eq 23). For OA_knee the LDSC regression uses the per-SNP N column (range 10,167–1,316,504; §2.1, §3.7) rather than a single constant; because r_g = gencov/√(h₁h₂) is mathematically independent of N (§5.2), the representative N affects only OA_knee's own h² (which scales as 1/N, Table 1 †), not any r_g, MR direction or conclusion. A full numerical sensitivity table across representative N = 10,167–1,316,504 is given in Supplement S3 and plotted in Supplementary Figure S5.

### 3.2 Three blocks in the genetic-correlation matrix

The 13×13 r_g matrix showed three blocks (Figure 1; Table 2). The ten BMD sites were strongly correlated with one another (r_g 0.69–0.96, mean 0.81), and anatomically adjacent sites were nearly identical (spine–lumbar +0.96, trunk–pelvis +0.96, trunk–spine +0.96). Both OA definitions correlated positively with all ten BMD sites (knee OA +0.14 to +0.19; self-reported OA +0.12 to +0.25), and the largest self-reported OA correlations were with the femoral sites (+0.23 femoral neck, +0.25 femoral total), consistent with self-reported OA including hip OA, which relates more strongly to femoral BMD. The positivity of the OA×BMD block (20/20 significant; Table 2) and the OA_knee×OA_selfrep r_g = +0.84 are both contingent on the global Z-sign correction applied to OA_selfrep (Supplement S2); without it the OA_selfrep effects invert, so the block's signs depend on that correction, which is justified by the GDF5/FTO direction evidence and the +0.84 OA–OA agreement (§2.4). The two OA definitions correlated at r_g = +0.84 (SE 0.018).

Fracture provided a validation block. Any-fracture correlated negatively with every BMD site (r_g −0.45 to −0.60; all q_FDR < 0.001), reproducing the established inverse BMD–fracture relationship, and positively but not significantly with both OA definitions (+0.15 and +0.21; q_FDR = 0.33 and 0.37).

Of the 78 pairs, 76 were significant at BH-FDR q<0.05 and 75 at Bonferroni. Two pairs were non-significant under both criteria — the two OA–fracture pairs, the expected null — and a third, fracture with femoral-neck BMD (r_g = −0.58, p = 8.1×10⁻⁴), fell below the Bonferroni threshold (p_Bonf = 0.063) while remaining significant at FDR (q = 8.3×10⁻⁴); it is the weakest of the ten BMD–fracture correlations but retains the expected negative sign.

### 3.3 MR positive control: BMD protects against fracture

Two-sample MR using 31 independent instruments for total-body BMD showed that genetically predicted higher BMD reduces fracture risk: IVW β = −0.337 per SD (SE 0.022, p = 3.7×10⁻⁵¹), equivalent to OR = 1.40 (95% CI 1.34–1.46) for fracture per 1-SD lower BMD. The estimate held under random effects (p = 2.4×10⁻¹²), the MR-Egger intercept was null (p = 0.558), the weighted median agreed (−0.364 per SD; strict-clumped set, as in Table 3), and leave-one-out produced no sign reversal. Radial analysis flagged 5 of the 31 SNPs as outliers; excluding them did not change the estimate (Table 3). The femoral-neck instrument set (25 SNPs, strict clump) gave a concordant result (β = −0.351 per SD; OR = 1.42, 95% CI 1.34–1.51 per SD lower), and the conservative 1-Mb sensitivity set (26 SNPs) gave β = −0.366 (OR = 1.44, 95% CI 1.36–1.53); the two clumping strategies agree to within 1.5%. These magnitudes also agree with the published GEFOS estimate (OR = 1.55, 95% CI 1.48–1.63 per SD lower femoral-neck BMD, Trajanoska et al. 2018), obtained in an overlapping-but-larger sample that includes a UKB component (§3.7). The positive control is therefore not fully independent of the BMD exposure — GEFOS incorporates a UKB component that overlaps the wholly-UKB BMD sample — but its agreement in direction and magnitude with our reconstructed scale supports pipeline validity. For the total-body BMD→fracture pair the MR-Egger intercept is null (p=0.558), so the IVW magnitude is unconfounded by directional pleiotropy. For the femoral-neck BMD→fracture pair the MR-Egger intercept is significant (p=0.018, Table 3), indicating some directional pleiotropy; the MR-Egger slope (−0.343) and weighted median (−0.365) remain strongly protective and close to the IVW estimate, so the protective conclusion is robust to pleiotropy, although the IVW point estimate may be modestly inflated and should be read with that caveat.

### 3.4 Genetically predicted BMD causally increases knee OA risk

β is per 1-SD higher BMD on the standardised (reconstructed) outcome scale; knee-OA slopes are on the linear-model scale of the source GWAS and are not odds ratios and are not exponentiated (§3.5, Figure 6). A linear rather than logit link is retained because the source analysis reports effects on that observed linear scale; converting to an odds ratio would require assuming a population prevalence K and a logit link that the source GWAS did not use, and would rescale the effect without changing its direction, significance or power (|β|/SE is scale-invariant; §3.7). The p-values are unaffected by the sample-scale correction in §3.7. The β and n_snp shown are the strict-clumped primary estimates; full statistics, including the reverse and null pairs, are in Table 3.

Five lines of evidence support a causal interpretation. The direction was consistent across all five exposures; MR-Egger intercepts were all p>0.24, arguing against directional horizontal pleiotropy; weighted-median estimates matched the sign of the IVW estimate; leave-one-out produced no sign reversals, so no single SNP drives the result; and the direction agrees with the LDSC r_g (+0.12 to +0.25). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 68–86% across the five BMD→OA pairs), and under random-effects IVW only two of the five exposures remained significant at p<0.05 — total-body (p=0.006) and lumbar-spine (p=0.017) — whereas femoral-neck (p=0.055), arms (p=0.070) and legs (p=0.098) fell just outside it. High heterogeneity makes fixed-effect IVW p-values anti-conservative, so we do not rest the causal claim on them; nor do we rest it on the random-effects model, which is the appropriate summary under heterogeneity but leaves three of five pairs at the margin of significance. We therefore base the causal reading on evidence that is robust to both heterogeneity and pleiotropy: the consistency of direction across all five exposures, the null MR-Egger intercepts (p>0.24), the sign-concordant weighted-median estimates, and the fact that all five effects remained positively causal after MR-PRESSO outlier removal, with four of five strengthening (§3.6). The fixed-effect p-values (all five ≤6.4×10⁻⁴) and the random-effects ones (2 of 5 <0.05) are reported together as a sensitivity pair rather than as the basis of the claim, and readers should note that a strict random-effects significance criterion is not met by three of the five exposures. Per-SNP scatter plots with IVW, MR-Egger and weighted-median fits are shown in Figure 6c for total-body BMD and in Supplementary Figure S1a–d for the remaining exposures.

### 3.5 Reverse MR and OA→fracture are non-significant and underpowered

In reverse MR, knee OA as exposure gave non-significant effects on total-body BMD (β=+0.106, p=0.33) and femoral-neck BMD (β=+0.142, p=0.19), and no significant causal effect on fracture (β=+0.092, p=0.40). These three estimates are reported on the reconstructed scale (the same scale as the per-SNP slopes in Figure 6); only their direction and p-values can be interpreted, because the BMD-outcome reconstruction shares the N uncertainty of the forward exposure. The per-SNP scatter for knee OA→total-body BMD (Figure 6d) is flat, with no apparent pleiotropic structure (MR-Egger intercepts p=0.476, 0.376 and 0.169 across the three null pairs, all non-significant; Table 3), and the remaining null pairs are in Supplementary Figure S1e–f. Together with the null OA–fracture r_g, these results are consistent with the well-powered forward MR (BMD→OA) capturing the main causal direction; the reverse OA→BMD estimate was null but underpowered, so a smaller reverse causal contribution is not excluded. These three null estimates are, however, weakly powered (achieved power 0.14–0.26; §3.8): they exclude large effects but not moderate ones, and are better described as an absence of evidence than as evidence of absence.

### 3.6 MR-PRESSO global (distortion) test

To test whether the MR conclusions depended on pleiotropic outliers or on the clumping strategy, we repeated the full MR analysis with strict PLINK-equivalent clumping (1000G Phase 3 EUR, r²<0.001 over 10 Mb; §2.6) and applied the MR-PRESSO global (distortion) test (Verbanck et al. 2018) to all ten pairs (`mr_presso_results.csv` and Supplement). Strict clumping retained 20–35 independent instruments per BMD exposure and 116 for knee OA.

The distortion test was significant for nine of the ten pairs (p = 1.8×10⁻⁵ to 1.6×10⁻²¹), as expected from the high Cochran I² in §3.4–3.5, and identified 0–3 outlier instruments per pair (0 only for OA_knee→femoral-neck BMD). Supplementary Figure S7 summarises heterogeneity (I²), directional pleiotropy (MR-Egger intercept tests) and outlier counts across all ten pairs; Figure 7 shows the four headline funnels. Removing outliers did not reverse any effect direction, and the strict-clumped estimates reproduced those from the conservative 1-Mb clumping:

- BMD→fracture (positive control): total-body β_IVW −0.337 → −0.352 after outlier removal (K=31; distortion p=9.4×10⁻⁹); femoral-neck β_IVW −0.350 → −0.345 (K=25; p=3.4×10⁻¹²). These are on the N-corrected scale (§3.7). The distortion test, like every direction and reversal conclusion here, is N-scale-invariant, so the p-values hold on either the raw reconstructed or the corrected scale.
- BMD→knee OA: all five exposures remained positively causal, and four of five strengthened after outlier removal (total-body +0.023 → +0.026, K=31; femoral-neck +0.017 → +0.021, K=25; lumbar-spine +0.028 → +0.033, K=21; arms +0.022 → +0.026, K=16). Legs attenuated slightly but stayed positive (+0.018 → +0.015, K=24).
- Null pairs: OA_knee→fracture (K=69, β +0.092) and OA_knee→BMD (K=69, β +0.106 total / +0.142 femoral-neck) remained non-significant after outlier removal. For OA_knee→femoral-neck BMD no outliers were detected (distortion p=1.0); for the other two null pairs the distortion test was significant (p<2×10⁻⁴) but removing the flagged outliers did not change the non-significant direction. Outlier removal did, however, move these point estimates substantially within their wide intervals — OA_knee→fracture +0.092 → +0.200 (2 outliers removed), OA_knee→total-body BMD +0.106 → +0.048 (1 removed) — which we report for transparency: the null pairs are unstable in magnitude as well as imprecise, reinforcing that they carry no usable causal information (§3.8).

Finally, the reverse-direction estimates are sensitive to instrument selection as well as imprecise: for OA_knee→total-body BMD the conservative 1-Mb clumping window gives p = 0.097 and MRlap's 156-instrument set gives p = 0.013 (§2.8), against p = 0.33 under the primary strict clumping. None reaches significance and MRlap's standard errors are anti-conservative (§2.8), so we report the most conservative values, but the spread is reported here because it qualifies how much weight the reverse direction can bear. The primary causal findings therefore withstand both pleiotropic outliers and the choice of clumping strategy: BMD protects against fracture, BMD raises knee OA risk, and neither OA→BMD nor OA→fracture is supported. The significant MR findings (the BMD→fracture positive control and the five BMD→OA pairs) all remain significant after Bonferroni correction for the ten MR pairs tested (α = 0.005; `mr_results.csv`), so the conclusions are not artefacts of multiple testing; the three null pairs are non-significant by construction and should be read with their power (§3.8). MR-PRESSO funnel plots for the four headline pairs, with outliers flagged individually, are shown in Figure 7; leave-one-out stability for all ten pairs is in Supplementary Figure S2.

### 3.7 Sample-scale correction and external validation against GEFOS

Munged summary statistics contain only Z, MAF and N, so effect sizes were reconstructed as SE = 1/√(N·2·MAF·(1−MAF)) and β = Z·SE. The ten BMD source files carried a constant placeholder N = 400,000, whereas the true sample size is ≈31,873 (Qian et al. 2025). Because β scales as 1/√N, the placeholder N was 400,000/31,873 ≈ 12.55 times too large, which shrank the reconstructed BMD β to √(31,873/400,000) = 0.2823 of the true SD-scale effect; they were underestimated 3.5426-fold. In forward MR the BMD is the exposure and enters the denominator of the ratio β_outcome/β_BMD, so the raw MR β is 0.2823× of the true scale and the true-scale forward MR β is recovered by multiplying by √(31,873/400,000) = 0.2823 (equivalently dividing by 3.5426): BMD_total→fracture β = −0.337 (OR 1.40), within 8–10% of the published GEFOS estimate of 1.55. In reverse MR the BMD is the outcome; its reconstructed β shares the same N uncertainty, so the reverse MR β is reported on the reconstructed scale (the same scale as the per-SNP slopes in Figure 6) and, as noted in §3.5, only its direction and p-value are interpreted. Since β and its SE scale identically, all z-statistics, p-values, effect directions and significance are unchanged; only the absolute magnitudes required correction (Table 3, §3.3). OA_knee needed no correction: its N varies per SNP (107,795 distinct values, range 10,167–1,316,504), as expected for a meta-analysis with correct per-SNP N.

After correction, genetically predicted BMD gives OR = 1.40 (95% CI 1.34–1.46) for fracture per 1-SD lower total-body BMD and OR = 1.42 (95% CI 1.34–1.51) for femoral-neck BMD (strict-clumped primary; the 1-Mb sensitivity gave 1.40 and 1.44). The GEFOS consortium reported OR = 1.55 (95% CI 1.48–1.63) per 1-SD lower femoral-neck BMD in 37,857 cases and 227,116 controls (Trajanoska et al. 2018); the two are compared directly in Supplementary Figure S3. Our estimates are 8–10% lower, with overlapping confidence intervals, which is close agreement given the different cohorts, fracture definitions and BMD measurement sites (total-body OR 1.40 is 9.6% below 1.55; femoral-neck OR 1.42 is 8.4% below). An overlapping-but-larger consortium thereby corroborates the pipeline: the direction matches and the magnitude falls within about 10%, which supports the biological plausibility of the reconstructed BMD effect scale but does not constitute external replication. The BMD sample size is taken from the source publication's per-site metadata (Qian et al. 2025), not inferred from the GEFOS comparison. Per-site N varies across the accession series (30,449–32,017; §2.1). Using site-specific correction factors instead of the unified √(400,000/31,873) = 3.5426 changes the absolute β by less than 0.3% for the headline estimates (total-body and femoral-neck BMD) and by at most 2.3% for the smallest site (lumbar spine, N=30,449), an order of magnitude below the 8–10% GEFOS agreement band. No rounded value in Table 3 changes (OR 1.40 / 1.42; all β to three decimals), and z-statistics, p-values, directions and significance are unaffected, being N-scale-invariant.

### 3.8 Statistical power of the MR estimates

A non-significant IVW estimate is informative only if the analysis could have detected an effect of a plausible size. We therefore computed, for every pair, the minimum detectable effect at 80% power with a two-sided α = 0.05, MDE = (1.96 + 0.84) × SE, together with the achieved power for the observed point estimate (Table 4; Figure 8; `mr_power.csv`, Supplementary). Because β and its SE scale identically, the ratio |β|/SE — and hence every power statement here — is invariant to the sample-scale correction of §3.7.

The seven pairs underlying the positive-control and BMD→OA findings were well powered: achieved power was 0.93–1.00, and even the smallest significant effect (femoral-neck BMD→knee OA, β = +0.017, SE = 0.005) retained 93% power. The three null pairs were not. With SE = 0.108 they could exclude only |β| ≥ 0.303, and the observed estimates (+0.092 for knee OA→fracture; +0.106 and +0.142 for knee OA→BMD) all lie inside that bound, with achieved power of 0.14, 0.17 and 0.26 respectively. On the log-odds scale of the fracture outcome the bound corresponds to OR ≈ 1.36 per unit of the exposure contrast as scaled in Table 3, subject to the scale caveats of §3.5 and §3.7.

This asymmetry governs interpretation. The BMD→fracture and BMD→OA conclusions rest on well-powered estimates and stand as reported. The reverse and OA→fracture results exclude large effects but are compatible with moderate ones in the direction of their own point estimates, so they should be read as an absence of evidence rather than as evidence of absence.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

Jointly analysing 13 skeletal phenotypes gave a consistent architecture: the BMD sites form a strongly inter-correlated cluster, OA correlates moderately and positively with every BMD site, fracture correlates negatively with every BMD site, and the two OA definitions correlate strongly with each other. MR resolves the direction for the well-powered pairs on the pleiotropy-robust evidence (§3.4) rather than on the fixed-effect p-values: genetically higher BMD raises knee OA risk. The reverse (OA→BMD) and OA→fracture estimates were non-significant and underpowered, so they exclude large effects but not moderate ones (§3.8).

### 4.2 Resolving the OA–BMD paradox

These findings resolve a long-standing clinical tension. High BMD protects against fracture, confirmed here as a positive control, yet the same variants that raise BMD also raise OA risk. The biology is consistent with this: subchondral bone sclerosis and osteophyte formation are hallmarks of OA and are localised bone-formation phenotypes, which may share regulatory programs with systemic bone accrual. That BMD protects against fracture yet raises OA risk implies that a single BMD-increasing genetic profile has opposing consequences at the two endpoints, so bone mass alone is not a sufficient proxy for skeletal health across endpoints. One mechanistic reading, offered as a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated pathway, is that shared effectors link systemic bone accrual to the localised bone-formation phenotypes of OA. The WNT16, SOX6 and GDF5 loci are each established for both BMD regulation and OA susceptibility — our lead instrument SNPs rs3779381 (WNT16), rs7108738 (SOX6) and rs224333 (GDF5) lie in these genes (§2.2) — so they are plausible candidate shared effectors; direct evidence that they mediate a profile raising BMD while promoting osteophyte and subchondral-sclerosis phenotypes that manifest as OA is, however, lacking and requires joint-specific functional validation.

A second, non-exclusive explanation must be stated because the present design cannot separate it. Areal BMD, as measured by DXA, is bone mineral content divided by projected bone area and is therefore confounded by skeletal size [18]: larger bones yield higher areal BMD at unchanged volumetric density, and larger joints may be more prone to OA through joint geometry rather than through bone material quantity. Under this reading, part of the positive OA–BMD genetic correlation, and part of the BMD→OA causal estimate, could reflect shared genetic determination of bone size rather than of bone mass or turnover. We could not exclude this pathway: the source GWAS report areal BMD, and no volumetric BMD, bone-area or femoral-geometry phenotype was available to condition on. The distinction matters clinically, because a size-driven component would contribute to fracture protection and to OA susceptibility in opposite directions and would not translate into a shared intervention target. Separating the size pathway from the mass pathway will require volumetric BMD or hip-geometry GWAS and conditional or multivariable MR; until then the BMD→OA effect should be read as an effect of the BMD phenotype as clinically measured, not as an effect of bone material quantity alone.

Site also matters for direction: Zhou et al. [5] reported that higher femoral-neck BMD *protected* against hip OA (a negative MR effect), whereas our ten-site analysis shows higher BMD *raises* knee-OA risk (positive). This reversal is a hip-versus-knee contrast rather than a contradiction of the literature — hip OA is a localised disease of a heavily weight-bearing joint whose subchondral bone responds to load differently from the knee — and it cautions against generalising any single BMD→OA estimate across joints. Direct measurements of bone quality (microarchitecture, turnover, cortical porosity) were not made here and are a direction for future work.

### 4.3 Why evidence for a genetic link from OA to fracture remains limited

Neither the genetic correlation (LDSC r_g = +0.15 and +0.21, both non-significant after FDR) nor Mendelian randomisation (β = +0.092, p = 0.395) supported a causal link from OA to fracture. Mendelian-randomisation studies of OA have overwhelmingly treated OA as the outcome, and where OA has been used as an exposure it has chiefly been in reverse-causality analyses within comorbidity studies [16,17]; to our knowledge the OA→fracture direction has not previously been tested directly, and the present analysis is the first such test. The consistency of the BMD→OA effect across the five sites is itself informative: the per-SD effects were tightly clustered (+0.017 to +0.028), and the weight-bearing femoral-neck site did not give a larger effect than the non-weight-bearing arms site (+0.017 vs +0.022). This pattern is more consistent with a systemic mechanism, whether operating through bone mass or through skeletal size (§4.2), than with a purely site-specific mechanical-load effect, but it is a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated finding: we did not formally test the weight-bearing-versus-non-weight-bearing difference, the 0.005 gap is within sampling error, and the reconstructed β scale widens the credible intervals, so the systemic interpretation remains suggestive. This site-consistency is a specific inference enabled by the ten-site design that the two-site studies of Hackinger (2017) and Zhou (2025) could not make. The MR component of this null is weakly powered (§3.8): it excludes |β| ≥ 0.30 but not the smaller effects compatible with its own point estimate, so it should not be read as excluding a moderate causal contribution. Patients with OA do sustain fractures, but our results provide no well-powered evidence that this burden is mediated by inherited differences in bone quantity; a moderate contribution cannot be excluded (§3.8). Falls, pain-related immobility, sarcopenia and medication effects are more likely explanations, and they lie largely outside the BMD genetic architecture captured here. The dissociation is itself informative: because the OA→fracture MR pairs were underpowered (§3.8), the present analysis cannot attribute OA's fracture burden to inherited differences in bone quantity; environmental and functional pathways (falls, immobility, sarcopenia, analgesia) remain plausible and warrant separate study.

### 4.4 Strengths

This analysis covers ten BMD sites rather than one or two, so site specificity can be assessed, and uses two OA definitions, which show consistent cross-site patterns. The BMD–fracture positive control sits inside the same framework and provides internal validation of allele orientation and pipeline correctness. Both Bonferroni and FDR correction are applied across all 78 pairs, and a common SNP set is used throughout, so the cells of the r_g matrix can be compared with one another.

### 4.5 Limitations

The absolute MR effect sizes are provisional. Exposure statistics were reconstructed from Z, N and MAF, so the β scale depends on the accuracy of the reported N. For the ten BMD datasets the placeholder N is inconsistent with the observed signal strength (mean χ² ≈ 1.2 and 170–514 genome-wide-significant SNPs, far fewer than n≈400,000 would produce); the corrected scale used here is the best estimate available, and the numerical effect sizes would need recomputing if different per-trait values were established. Directions, significance and robustness are unaffected by a uniform rescaling of exposure β. The primary analysis used strict PLINK-equivalent clumping (r²<0.001 over 10 Mb, 1000 Genomes Phase 3 EUR reference; §2.6), with the conservative 1-Mb window as a sensitivity analysis (§3.3, §3.6); MR-PRESSO confirmed that the primary causal directions withstand both pleiotropic outliers and the choice of clumping strategy. Strict-clumped MR underlies Table 3 and the GEFOS positive-control validation (§3.3, §3.7). Heterogeneity was high (I² = 68–86% for the BMD→OA pairs, 30–49% for the reverse/null pairs), under which two of five exposures remained significant under random-effects IVW (§3.4); because neither the fixed-effect nor the random-effects p-values carry the causal claim on their own, we rest it on the concordance of the pleiotropy-robust estimators — null MR-Egger intercepts, sign-concordant weighted medians and the outlier-free MR-PRESSO estimates (§3.4, §3.6). Instrument counts were 16–31 for the BMD exposures and 69 for knee OA after strict clumping and joining with the outcome files (pre-join counts 20–35 per BMD exposure and 116 for knee OA; §2.6), which limits the power of pleiotropy-robust methods, although MR-PRESSO and radial outlier removal reach the same conclusions. OA h² is reported on both observed and liability scales (Table 1; Lee et al. 2011). A further limitation is that every BMD phenotype is an areal (DXA) measurement, which is confounded by skeletal size (§4.2, [18]): part of the BMD→OA effect could reflect bone size rather than bone material quantity, and no volumetric BMD or bone-geometry phenotype was available with which to separate the two pathways. All 13 phenotypes are cited to their original publications (§2.1, References), and the sample sizes used (knee OA 1,208,397; BMD 31,873; OA_selfrep 462,933; fracture 264,973) are confirmed against those sources. The femoral-neck BMD→fracture positive-control pair shows a significant MR-Egger intercept (p=0.018), so its IVW magnitude may be inflated by directional pleiotropy or sample overlap (§2.8); the MR-Egger slope and weighted median remain strongly protective, and the total-body BMD→fracture pair (Egger p=0.558) is the primary positive control. The reverse and OA→fracture estimates are underpowered (achieved power 0.14–0.26; §3.8, Table 4) and can exclude only |β| ≥ 0.30, so moderate effects in the direction of their point estimates remain compatible with the data.

### 4.6 Conclusion

Genetically higher BMD protects against fracture and, on the pleiotropy-robust evidence, raises knee OA risk; both conclusions are drawn from estimators robust to heterogeneity and directional pleiotropy rather than from the fixed-effect IVW p-values (§3.4), and the BMD→OA effect pertains to the clinically measured areal-BMD phenotype, which may include a skeletal-size component that we could not separate (§4.2). We found no well-powered evidence of reverse causation or of a genetic pathway from OA to fracture, but those three estimates were underpowered and exclude only large effects (§3.8). The OA–BMD association is therefore bidirectional in genetic correlation, while the causal evidence is asymmetric — genetically higher BMD increasing knee-OA risk — with that asymmetry resting on the well-powered forward estimates and remaining provisional, both because the reverse estimates exclude large but not moderate effects and because residual sample overlap cannot be fully excluded as a contributor to the forward effect magnitude (§2.8, §3.8), so no well-powered evidence of a causal pathway from OA to fracture is available. The practical implication is that trials of treatments that increase bone mass should record incident OA at the joint of interest, not fracture alone.

### 5.2 Strategic note A — N-invariance of r_g and MR

The genetic-correlation matrix and all MR **directions, signs and significance levels are invariant to per-trait N** (r_g = gencov/√(h₁·h₂) depends only on the genotype–phenotype covariance structure, not on N or on M; empirically verified — varying fracture N over 150k–1,000,000 left every r_g unchanged to three decimal places). The only quantities that scale with N are the **absolute h² values** (h² ∝ 1/N) and the absolute MR β magnitudes when an exposure β is reconstructed from Z, N and MAF. **Conclusion:** the result framework (three-block architecture; BMD→fracture protection; BMD→OA causality; null OA→fracture / OA→BMD) stands *even before* fracture and OA_selfrep N are finally confirmed; only absolute numbers require updating once those N are locked.
